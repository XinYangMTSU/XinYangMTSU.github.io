Book One: The Gate of Numbers

Chapter Four: The Secret of Prime Numbers
"Some secrets become more mysterious the more you discover." — A Rule of Infinity Academy

The next morning, Ada woke before her alarm.

She wasn't thinking about school.

She wasn't thinking about piano practice.

She wasn't even thinking about Harry Potter.

She was thinking about one thing.

Prime Numbers

She had never imagined that numbers could belong to families.

Even stranger, she had never imagined that someone had discovered those families thousands of years ago.

She climbed out of bed, looked at the clock, and smiled.

6:31

Six.

Thirty-one.

"Thirty-one..."

She whispered the number to herself.

"Is thirty-one prime?"

She didn't know.

But now she wanted to find out.

After brushing her teeth with the hourglass, washing her face, eating breakfast, and getting dressed, Ada hurried toward the front door.

Mom noticed immediately.

"Someone seems excited today."

"I've been thinking."

Mom smiled.

"I had a feeling."

They climbed into the car.

As they drove through Murfreesboro, Ada looked out the window.

House numbers passed by.

Speed-limit signs.

License plates.

Everything still seemed full of numbers.

Finally she asked,

"Mom?"

"Yes?"

"Who discovered prime numbers?"

Mom was quiet for several seconds.

"That," she finally said, "is a wonderful question."

"So who was it?"

"No one knows."

Ada blinked.

"What do you mean?"

"People have known about prime numbers for thousands of years."

"So somebody must have found them."

"Probably many people."

Ada frowned.

"But somebody had to be first."

"Perhaps."

"But history doesn't always tell us."

Ada looked thoughtful.

"So..."

"Who made them famous?"

Mom smiled.

"Now that question has an answer."

"Oh?"

"A mathematician named Euclid."

"Who's Euclid?"

"He lived more than two thousand years ago."

Ada's eyes became wide.

"Two thousand?"

"Long before cars."

"Long before electricity."

"Long before airplanes."

"Long before America."

Ada tried to imagine someone living that long ago.

"What did he do?"

"He wrote one of the most important mathematics books ever written."

"A math book?"

Mom nodded.

"It was called Elements."

"People are still reading it today."

Ada looked surprised.

"A book can last two thousand years?"

"The very best books can."

Ada looked out the window again.

"So..."

"Did Euclid invent prime numbers?"

Mom laughed softly.

"No."

"He discovered something incredible about them."

"What?"

Mom glanced at Ada in the rear-view mirror.

"He proved something that nobody had ever proved before."

Ada leaned forward.

"What was it?"

Mom smiled.

"I'll tell you after school."

"Aww..."

"You always do that."

"What?"

"You make me wait."

Mom laughed.

"That's because discoveries are more exciting when you have time to think about them."

Ada crossed her arms.

"I don't like waiting."

"I know."

"But mathematicians wait all the time."

"For what?"

"For ideas."

The rest of the drive passed quietly.

During math class, Ada could hardly pay attention.

She kept thinking about Euclid.

Who was he?

How could someone from two thousand years ago still be teaching children today?

During recess she searched the school library.

The mathematics shelf was small.

Most of the books were about counting, multiplication, or geometry.

Then she found a thin children's book called

Great Mathematicians

She opened it carefully.

Near the beginning was a drawing of an old man with a beard, wearing a long white robe.

Below the picture were two words.

Euclid

Ada smiled.

"So that's you."

She began reading.

Euclid lived in Alexandria more than two thousand years ago.

He loved asking questions.

He believed that mathematics should never depend on guessing.

Everything should be proven.

Ada stopped reading.

"That sounds like Mom."

She smiled.

Then another sentence caught her attention.

Euclid proved that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

Ada froze.

Infinitely many?

She whispered the sentence again.

"There are infinitely many prime numbers."

She knew what infinity meant.

But how could there be infinitely many prime numbers?

That afternoon, she climbed into Mom's car almost before Mom had finished parking.

"I read about Euclid!"

Mom smiled.

"I thought you might."

"And it says there are infinitely many prime numbers."

"Yes."

"How did he know?"

Mom looked at Ada.

"Would you like to discover his idea yourself?"

Ada grinned.

"You already know my answer."

Mom smiled.

"I thought so."

When they arrived home, Mom walked into the kitchen instead of the playroom.

She took out a notebook.

A pencil.

And one small bowl of cherries.

"Today," she said, "we're going to think like Euclid."

Ada sat down immediately.

Mom wrote four numbers across the top of the paper.

2    3    5    7

"These are the first four prime numbers," Mom said.

Ada nodded.

"I know those."

Mom drew multiplication signs between them.

2 × 3 × 5 × 7

"Can you calculate it?"

Ada picked up her pencil.

Two times three is six.

Six times five is thirty.

Thirty times seven is...

She counted carefully.

210

Mom nodded.

"Good."

Then she wrote one more line.

210 + 1 = 211

Ada looked confused.

"Why did you add one?"

"Because that's exactly what Euclid imagined."

"He multiplied all the prime numbers he knew..."

"...and then added one."

Ada stared at the paper.

"So..."

"Is 211 prime?"

Mom smiled.

"I don't know."

"You don't?"

"No."

"Let's investigate."

Ada liked that word.

Investigate.

She tried dividing 211 by two.

There was a remainder.

Three?

No.

Five?

No.

Seven?

Still no.

She continued checking.

Finally she looked up.

"I don't think any of them work."

Mom nodded.

"That's right."

"So..."

"Two, three, five and seven cannot divide 211."

Ada smiled.

"Then 211 belongs to the prime family!"

"Yes."

Ada laughed.

"We found another one!"

Mom leaned back in her chair.

"Now imagine something."

"What?"

"Suppose someone tells you..."

"...'These are all the prime numbers that exist.'"

She pointed to

2, 3, 5, 7

"But then..."

"...we built 211."

Ada's smile slowly disappeared.

"Oh..."

"If 211 is prime..."

"...then the first list wasn't complete."

"Exactly."

Mom continued.

"What if we multiply every prime number we know..."

"...and add one again?"

Ada thought.

"We'll probably find another one."

"And after that?"

"Another."

"And after that?"

Ada looked down at the notebook.

Another.

Another.

Another.

She suddenly looked up.

"They never end."

Mom smiled.

"That is exactly what Euclid proved over two thousand years ago."

Ada sat quietly.

She wasn't amazed because Euclid was clever.

She was amazed because...

She had almost discovered the idea herself.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

Finally Ada whispered,

"So somewhere..."

"...there's another prime number nobody has ever discovered?"

Mom nodded.

"There are many."

"People are still discovering larger and larger prime numbers today."

Ada's eyes became wide.

"Really?"

"Yes."

"They use enormous computers."

"Sometimes the new prime number has millions of digits."

Ada laughed.

"Millions?"

"How could anyone even read it?"

Mom laughed too.

"They usually don't."

"They let computers help."

Ada looked back at the list.

2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19...

The dots at the end suddenly felt different.

Yesterday they simply meant "more numbers."

Today they meant

Forever.

Infinity.

Ada looked thoughtful.

"If prime numbers go on forever..."

"...why do people still care about them?"

Mom smiled.

"That's another wonderful question."

She reached into her pocket and placed her phone on the table.

"This phone uses prime numbers every day."

Ada looked surprised.

"My phone?"

Mom nodded.

"When I buy something online..."

"When I log into my bank..."

"When I send private information..."

"...prime numbers help keep everything safe."

Ada blinked.

"The Internet uses prime numbers?"

"It does."

"But why?"

Mom picked up two small padlocks from a drawer.

One was tiny.

The other was much larger.

She placed them beside each other.

"Imagine you have a treasure chest."

"You want everyone to be able to put letters inside..."

"...but only you can open it."

Ada nodded.

"That would be useful."

"The Internet has the same problem."

"Millions of people send messages every second."

"They buy books."

"They order food."

"They pay bills."

"They send photographs."

"They visit doctors online."

"They need a lock."

"A very special lock."

Ada picked up the larger padlock.

"And prime numbers help make that lock?"

"Exactly."

Mom drew two large circles.

Inside one she wrote

Prime Number A

Inside the other she wrote

Prime Number B

"Imagine these are enormous prime numbers."

"Not eleven."

"Not thirteen."

"Imagine numbers with hundreds of digits."

Ada laughed.

"I couldn't even write one!"

"Neither could I."

"But computers can."

Mom continued.

"If we multiply those two giant prime numbers together..."

"...we get one gigantic number."

"Multiplying them is easy."

"But..."

She paused.

"If someone only sees the giant number..."

"...finding the original two prime numbers is incredibly difficult."

Ada leaned closer.

"So..."

"Going forward is easy."

"Going backward is hard."

Mom smiled.

"Exactly."

"And that's why they help protect secrets."

Ada looked amazed.

"So..."

"The whole Internet trusts prime numbers?"

"In many ways, yes."

Ada looked down at her notebook.

Yesterday...

Prime numbers had simply been a family.

Today...

They were helping protect millions of people around the world.

She slowly wrote in her notebook.

Prime numbers are much more important than I thought.

Then she paused.

Another question appeared in her mind.

If people had been studying prime numbers for over two thousand years...

...were there still mysteries left?

She wrote carefully.

Are there still things nobody knows about prime numbers?

Mom glanced at the question.

"There are."

"A lot?"

Mom nodded.

"More than you can imagine."

"Some of the greatest mathematicians in history..."

"...spent their entire lives studying prime numbers."

"And some of their questions are still unanswered."

Ada looked surprised.

"So..."

"I could still discover something new?"

Mom smiled warmly.

"You absolutely could."

Ada became very quiet.

She imagined herself growing older.

She imagined books.

Computers.

Blackboards filled with equations.

Perhaps...

One day...

She might discover something that nobody else had ever known.

That evening, after piano practice, Ada returned to the reading room.

She opened her notebook.

Beneath yesterday's questions she carefully added:

Prime numbers never end.

Then she added one final sentence.

Maybe someday I will discover one that no one has ever seen before.

She smiled.

Then she closed the notebook.

The reading room became quiet.

The warm light from the reading lamp spread across the couch.

Several seconds passed.

Nothing happened.

Then...

Very slowly...

The mysterious book on the bookshelf opened by itself.

The pages turned.

One.

Two.

Three.

Finally they stopped.

A single page began glowing with soft golden light.

At the very top appeared three familiar words.

THE GATE OF NUMBERS

Beneath the title...

A golden doorway slowly appeared.

It was closed.

Around its edge were carved dozens of tiny prime numbers.

2

3

5

7

11

13

17

19

They shimmered gently like tiny stars.

Then...

A sentence appeared beneath the door.

WHEN THE STUDENT IS READY, THE FIRST GATE WILL OPEN.

The golden letters slowly faded.

The book quietly closed.

Across the room, Ada had already fallen asleep on the couch, Harry Potter resting open beside her.

She never saw the doorway.

Not yet.
