Where Every Great Discovery Begins with a Question

Book One: The Gate of Numbers

Chapter Two: Numbers Everywhere
“Sometimes the world changes only because you have begun to notice it.” — The Second Rule of Infinity Academy

Ada woke the next morning before Mom called her.

For a few seconds, she remained beneath her blanket, looking at the pale morning light around the edges of her bedroom curtains.

Something important had happened the night before.

She remembered sitting beneath the large reading lamp. She remembered talking with Mom about bicycles, piano, numbers, and infinity. She remembered writing a name across the top of a clean page in her drawing notebook:

INFINITY ACADEMY

Then she remembered the question she had written beneath it:

Can everything be explained by mathematics?

Ada sat up.

Had the page behind her really turned by itself?

She was no longer certain.

Perhaps the air conditioner had come on.

Perhaps one of the books had been left open.

Perhaps she had only imagined it because she had been reading about magic.

She reached for the clock beside her bed.

The glowing numbers read:

6:42

Ada stared at them.

Usually, the clock simply told her whether she needed to get up. That morning, however, the numbers seemed to be doing something more.

The six told her the hour.

The four and the two told her the minutes.

Three small numbers were telling her exactly where she was in the morning.

“Numbers,” Ada whispered.

She climbed out of bed and opened her bedroom door.

The hallway was quiet. Mom and Dad were already awake downstairs, but neither of them had called for her yet.

Ada passed the thermostat.

It displayed:

72°

Another number.

She continued toward the kitchen.

The microwave clock read:

6:46

The refrigerator had numbers beside the temperature controls.

The oven had numbered buttons.

The milk carton had a date printed near the top.

Even the egg carton had a number showing how many eggs it could hold.

Mom was standing at the stove, frying an egg.

“Good morning,” she said. “You’re up early.”

“Mom?”

“Yes?”

“Why are there so many numbers in the kitchen?”

Mom looked around.

“Are there?”

Ada pointed.

“The microwave has numbers. The oven has numbers. The milk has numbers. The egg carton has numbers. Even the refrigerator has numbers.”

Mom smiled.

“Maybe the numbers were always there.”

“I know they were always there.”

“Then what changed?”

Ada considered this.

“I started noticing them.”

Mom placed a fried egg and a glass of milk on the table.

Beside them was a small bowl of cherries.

Ada counted the cherries.

12

“Did you put exactly twelve cherries in here?” Ada asked.

“No,” Mom said. “I just added a handful.”

Ada looked at the cherries.

“So twelve happened by accident?”

“Maybe.”

“Or maybe it wasn’t an accident.”

Mom raised an eyebrow.

“What else could it be?”

Ada divided the cherries into two equal groups.

“Six and six.”

Then she moved them again.

“Four, four, and four.”

Then once more.

“Three groups of four.”

Mom watched her.

“What are you doing?”

“Finding different ways to build twelve.”

Mom nodded.

“Just like factors.”

Ada looked up.

She had not thought of that.

The cherries were no longer only breakfast. They had become a picture of the number twelve.

Ada arranged them into two rows of six.

Then three rows of four.

Then four rows of three.

Each arrangement looked different, but the total never changed.

“Twelve has a lot of shapes,” Ada said.

“A number can sometimes be seen in many different ways,” Mom replied.

Ada ate one cherry.

Now there were eleven.

She tried to arrange the remaining cherries into equal rows.

Two rows did not work.

Three rows did not work.

Four rows did not work.

Five rows did not work either.

There was always one cherry left over.

Ada frowned.

“Eleven doesn’t want to make a rectangle.”

Mom laughed softly.

“Maybe eleven has different rules.”

Ada remembered her question from the car:

Why do some numbers have lots of factors, but some numbers hardly have any?

Before she could ask more, Mom looked at the time.

“We need to get ready for school.”

Ada ate the remaining cherries, although she saved the last one for several seconds before finally putting it in her mouth.

Eleven seemed more interesting than it had yesterday.

Upstairs, Ada placed the small hourglass beside the sink.

She turned it over and began brushing her teeth.

The sand slowly fell from the top of the hourglass to the bottom.

When the last grain dropped, two minutes had passed.

Ada looked at the hourglass.

It had measured time without using a clock.

Another kind of mathematics, she thought.

She chose a shirt labeled size eight.

Her shoes had numbers printed inside them.

Her backpack zipper had a tiny number stamped onto the metal.

She had never noticed that before.

When she returned downstairs, Mom was waiting beside the door.

“Ready?”

“Almost.”

Ada hurried into the playroom and found her drawing notebook beneath two books on the couch.

She opened it.

The page looked exactly as she remembered.

At the top:

INFINITY ACADEMY

Beneath it:

Every great discovery begins with a question.

And below that:

Can everything be explained by mathematics?

Nothing glowed.

No pages moved.

There was no sign of magic.

Ada closed the notebook and slipped it into her backpack.

On the drive to school, she watched numbers pass by the car window.

House numbers.

Speed-limit signs.

License plates.

Gas prices.

Road numbers.

Business signs displayed opening times and phone numbers.

The car dashboard showed the outside temperature, the speed, the time, and the number of miles remaining before the gas tank would be empty.

“Mom?”

“Yes?”

“How does the car know how many miles we can drive?”

“It estimates based on how much gas is left and how quickly we have been using it.”

“So it doesn’t know exactly?”

“No. It makes a prediction.”

“With numbers?”

“With numbers.”

Ada watched the estimated distance change from 278 miles to 277.

“Numbers can tell us about the future?”

“Sometimes they help us make a good guess.”

Ada leaned back in her seat.

Yesterday, numbers had seemed like things written on worksheets.

Today they seemed to be measuring the entire world.

At school, Ada noticed the room number beside the classroom door.

She noticed the clock above the whiteboard.

She noticed the date written in the corner.

She noticed page numbers in her reading book, numbered cubbies along the wall, and numbered spaces on the classroom calendar.

During attendance, the teacher counted how many students were present.

During spelling, the class was told to write ten sentences.

During science, they measured the length of a leaf.

During lunch, the cashier entered a number into a small screen.

During recess, Ada counted seven children on the swings, four playing tag, and six drawing with sidewalk chalk.

Her friend came over.

“What are you doing?” she asked.

“Counting.”

“Why?”

“I’m trying to see how many numbers are around us.”

Her friend looked around.

“I don’t see any numbers.”

Ada pointed toward the basketball court.

“There are numbers on the scoreboard.”

Then she pointed toward the building.

“There’s a number on the door.”

She pointed to a child’s shirt.

“There’s a number on that jersey.”

Her friend shrugged.

“Those are just numbers.”

Just numbers.

Ada thought about those words for the rest of recess.

How could something be “just a number” if it could describe time, distance, temperature, music, money, age, pages, speed, and almost everything else?

That afternoon, the teacher wrote a large number on the board:

12

“Today,” she said, “we are going to review factors.”

Ada sat straighter.

The teacher drew twelve dots.

“Who can arrange these into equal rows?”

Several students raised their hands.

“One row of twelve,” someone said.

The teacher drew it.

“Two rows of six,” said another student.

“Three rows of four,” Ada added.

The teacher nodded.

“So the factor pairs of twelve are one and twelve, two and six, and three and four.”

Ada thought about the cherries.

The teacher erased the board and wrote:

11

“What about eleven?”

The room became quiet.

“One row of eleven,” Ada said.

“Anything else?”

Ada imagined the cherries again.

Every attempt had left one cherry alone.

“No,” she said. “Only one and eleven.”

The teacher smiled.

“That makes eleven a special kind of number.”

Ada waited for her to continue.

But the school bell rang.

The teacher closed the math book.

“We’ll talk more about that another day.”

Ada stared at the number eleven still written on the board.

A special kind of number.

She wanted to know what made it special.

She wanted to know how many other numbers followed the same rule.

She wanted to know whether the numbers could recognize one another.

Most of all, she wanted to know why nobody had told her before that numbers could have personalities.

When Mom picked her up that afternoon, Ada climbed into the car and fastened her seat belt.

“How was school?” Mom asked.

“Good.”

Mom waited.

Ada smiled.

“Eleven is special.”

“Oh?”

“It can only be made with one and eleven.”

“That is interesting.”

“But my teacher didn’t tell us what kind of number it is.”

“Maybe she wants you to wonder about it.”

Ada looked out the window.

The numbers were still everywhere.

But now one of them seemed to be waiting for her.

That evening, after piano practice, Ada returned to the couch beneath the reading lamp.

She opened her drawing notebook.

Below her first question, she carefully wrote:

Why are numbers everywhere?

Then she added another:

What makes eleven special?

She studied the two questions.

They were smaller than:

Can everything be explained by mathematics?

But somehow, they felt like steps toward it.

Ada closed the notebook and placed it on the couch.

She returned to her piano to play one passage again before bedtime.

The notebook remained beneath the reading lamp.

For several minutes, nothing happened.

Then something slipped quietly from between its pages.

It was smaller than a grain of sand.

It shimmered once in the warm light.

A tiny golden speck.

It drifted onto the couch and disappeared between the threads of the fabric.

Across the room, one of the books on the shelf opened by itself.

On its empty first page, a single number slowly appeared:

11
